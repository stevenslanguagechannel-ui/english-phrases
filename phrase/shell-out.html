shell out

Phrasal verbLevel B2CasualEveryday life

To spend money on something, often when you don't really want to.

I had to shell out a lot of money for a new washing machine.

Tip: Use 'shell out (for/on) something' or 'shell out + amount'; it often suggests the money felt like a lot or wasn't a happy choice, so avoid it for small or pleasant spending.

Hear it in conversation

OlenaDid you finally get tickets for your cousin's wedding abroad?
KofiYes, but the flights were crazy expensive this close to the date.
OlenaOh no. How much did you end up paying?
KofiNearly nine hundred. I had to shell out for a last-minute seat, and then the hotel on top.
OlenaOuch. At least it's only once in a while.
KofiTrue. I'll be eating pasta at home for the next month, though.

Workbook

1. What does it mean?

In this conversation, what does 'shell out' mean?

2. Fill the gap

I didn't want to, but I had to a small fortune to get my laptop repaired before the deadline.

3. Choose the best reply

Amira: My landlord says the whole flat needs new windows, and we have to split the cost.

4. Your turn: speak

Tell me about a time you had to spend more money than you expected on something.

Answer out loud in 2–3 sentences and use the phrase. Then compare with the example answer.

Show an example answer

Last year my bike was stolen, and I had to shell out nearly four hundred euros for a new one because I need it to get to work. I wasn't happy about it, but I couldn't manage without it. Since then I've bought a much better lock!

Answer key: shell out

  1. 1. A. Pay a large amount of money, even though you'd prefer not to
  2. 2. shell out
  3. 3. B. That's annoying. I bet you'll have to shell out a few hundred each.
  4. 4. Example: Last year my bike was stolen, and I had to shell out nearly four hundred euros for a new one because I need it to get to work. I wasn't happy about it, but I couldn't manage without it. Since then I've bought a much better lock!
