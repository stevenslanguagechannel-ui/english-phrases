People & relationships

A conversational idiom workbook: 15 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. bang on about: To talk about the same subject for too long, in a boring or annoying way.
  2. break the ice: To make people feel relaxed when they first meet.
  3. claim to fame: The one interesting or impressive thing a person or place is known for.
  4. keep up appearances: To act as if everything is fine or normal, even when it isn't.
  5. lend a hand: To help someone with a job.
  6. make it up to you: To do something nice to fix a problem you caused.
  7. meet halfway: To reach a compromise where each side gives a little.
  8. pick up on: To notice something small or hidden, like a feeling or a change.
  9. reach out to: To contact someone, often to offer or ask for help or just to say hello.
  10. see someone off: To go with someone to the place they are leaving from, so you can say goodbye.
  11. spill the beans: To tell a secret by accident.
  12. tag along: To go somewhere with other people who didn't ask you.
  13. take a rain check: To say no to an invitation now and suggest doing it another time.
  14. water under the bridge: Something unpleasant that happened in the past and is no longer important.
  15. write off: To decide that someone or something is not worth your time or has no chance.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

bang on about

Phrasal verbLevel B2Casual

To talk about the same subject for too long, in a boring or annoying way.

Tip: Use it with 'about' plus the subject (bang on about politics, bang on about work); it's mildly critical, so say it about other people more than to their face. It's also common in the form 'go banging on about'.

AmiraHow was the train ride with Kofi? Six hours is a long time.
KenHonestly, I lost the will to live around hour two.
AmiraWhy, what happened?
KenHe wouldn't stop banging on about his new running app. Steps, routes, heart rate, everything.
AmiraHa, I'd have put my headphones on straight away.
KenI did, eventually. He didn't even notice.

break the ice

IdiomLevel B1Neutral

To make people feel relaxed when they first meet.

Tip: Use it as a verb phrase: 'break the ice with someone' or 'to break the ice'; it is common before a first meeting, a class, or a team event.

AmiraI'm nervous about the first day of my evening class tomorrow. I won't know anyone.

AmiraThat's a nice idea. People always chat over snacks.
KofiExactly. Then you can ask what everyone is studying.
AmiraOkay, I'll stop at the shop on my way home tonight.

OlenaOkay, now I want to visit.

keep up appearances

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

To act as if everything is fine or normal, even when it isn't.

Tip: Use it as a verb phrase, often with 'to' or after 'trying': 'We kept up appearances for the kids'; it often suggests hiding a problem, so don't use it for simply being polite.

AmiraHow was dinner with Ken's parents on Saturday?
OlenaHonestly, awful. Ken and I had argued all afternoon.
AmiraOh no. Did they notice?
OlenaI don't think so. We kept up appearances until dessert, then we both went quiet.
AmiraThat sounds exhausting. Have you two talked since?
OlenaYes, last night. We're fine now, just tired.

OlenaGreat. Pick any place you like.

meet halfway

Phrasal verbLevel B1Neutral

To reach a compromise where each side gives a little.

Tip: Use it as 'meet someone halfway (on something)' or 'meet halfway'; both sides give a little, so it often follows a disagreement about plans, money or time.

OlenaKofi, I really need the report by Thursday, but you said Monday.
KofiThursday is tough. I'm still waiting on the sales figures.
OlenaCould we meet halfway? How about Friday morning?
KofiFriday morning works. I'll send you what I have on Thursday evening, too.
OlenaPerfect, that helps a lot. Thanks.

reach out to

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To contact someone, often to offer or ask for help or just to say hello.

Tip: Use 'reach out to + person' (or just 'reach out' when the person is clear); it sounds friendly and is common at work and with friends, and don't confuse it with the physical 'reach out your hand'.

OlenaHave you heard from Kofi lately? He moved to Toronto in the spring.
AmiraNot really. I keep meaning to message him, but I never know what to say.
OlenaJust say hi. I'm sure he'd love to hear from you.
AmiraYou're right. I'll reach out to him this weekend and ask how the new job is going.
OlenaGood idea. Tell him we should all have a video call soon.

see someone off

Phrasal verbLevel B1Neutral

To go with someone to the place they are leaving from, so you can say goodbye.

Tip: Use it with a person in the middle: 'see someone off' (see him off, see them off); don't say 'see off' without an object, and don't confuse it with 'see someone out' (to the door).

OlenaIs your cousin's train still at six tomorrow?
KofiYes, it leaves at ten past. I'm going to the station early to see her off.
OlenaThat's nice. Will you carry her bags too?
KofiOf course. She has two huge suitcases and a long journey ahead.
OlenaSay hi from me. I hope she has a good trip.
KofiI will. She'll be back for the holidays anyway.

spill the beans

IdiomLevel B2Casual

To tell a secret by accident.

Tip: Use it as a verb phrase about the person who lets the secret out ("She spilled the beans"); it's often used when the leak is accidental, and it's usually followed by "about" plus the secret.

AmiraDid you tell Olena we're moving her desk next week? It's meant to be a surprise for her birthday.
KenNot yet. I nearly did at lunch, though.
AmiraPlease be careful. Last time you were in charge of a surprise, you spilled the beans in about five minutes.
KenThat was a one-off! I'll just talk about the weather if she asks.
AmiraGood plan. I'll bring the cake, you keep quiet.

tag along

Phrasal verbLevel B2Casual

To go somewhere with other people who didn't ask you.

Tip: Use 'tag along' (often with 'with' someone) when you join a plan uninvited or when you're happy for someone to join yours; it sounds friendly, but don't use it for formal invitations.

OlenaAre you doing anything on Saturday? I'm driving out to the lake with a few people from work.
KofiNothing special. I was going to clean the flat, but that can wait.
OlenaYou should come! Nobody will mind if you tag along. We're leaving around ten.
KofiAre you sure? I don't really know anyone else.
OlenaYou'll know me, and Amira is bringing her brother, so you won't be the only new face.
KofiOkay, great. I'll bring snacks for the car.

take a rain check

IdiomLevel B2Casual

To say no to an invitation now and suggest doing it another time.

Tip: Say 'Can I take a rain check?' or 'I'll take a rain check' to decline politely now while showing you still want to do it later; don't use it if you never want to go.

OlenaWe're all going to the new noodle place after class. Are you coming, Kofi?
KofiI'd love to, but I have to finish this report tonight.
OlenaOh, that's a shame. It won't be the same without you.
KofiI know! I'll take a rain check, if that's okay. Next week, maybe?
OlenaOf course. I'll book a table for next Friday.

water under the bridge

IdiomLevel B2Casual

Something unpleasant that happened in the past and is no longer important.

Tip: Use it after a conflict or mistake to say it no longer matters; it's often said as 'that's water under the bridge' or 'it's all water under the bridge now'.

AmiraKofi, I'm sorry again about missing your moving day. I felt awful.
KofiAmira, honestly, don't worry. It's water under the bridge.
AmiraReally? You were pretty annoyed at the time.
KofiI was, but we got everything done in the end.
AmiraThanks. Let me bring dinner to the new flat on Friday.
KofiDeal. And bring a big appetite.

write off

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To decide that someone or something is not worth your time or has no chance.

Tip: Use it with a person or thing as the object: 'write someone off' or 'write it off'. A pronoun goes in the middle, so say 'write him off', not 'write off him'.

OlenaDid you see Kofi's first draft for the client pitch? It's pretty rough.
KenI did. Honestly, I was ready to write it off.
OlenaReally? He's only had two days to work on it.
KenTrue. And his last project started badly too, then it turned out great.
OlenaExactly. Let's give him until Friday and then look again.
KenFine. I'll send him some notes this afternoon.
