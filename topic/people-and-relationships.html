People & relationships

A conversational idiom workbook: 23 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. bang on about: To talk about the same subject for too long, in a boring or annoying way.
  2. break the ice: To make people feel relaxed when they first meet.
  3. chip in: To give a small amount of money or help together with others.
  4. claim to fame: The one interesting or impressive thing a person or place is known for.
  5. drop by: To visit someone for a short, informal time.
  6. get along with: To have a friendly relationship with someone.
  7. hit it off: To like each other immediately when you meet.
  8. keep up appearances: To act as if everything is fine or normal, even when it isn't.
  9. lend a hand: To help someone with a job.
  10. make it up to you: To do something nice to fix a problem you caused.
  11. make yourself at home: Relax and feel comfortable in someone's house.
  12. meet halfway: To reach a compromise where each side gives a little.
  13. the more the merrier: It's good to have more people join us.
  14. pick up on: To notice something small or hidden, like a feeling or a change.
  15. reach out to: To contact someone, often to offer or ask for help or just to say hello.
  16. rub someone the wrong way: If something rubs you the wrong way, it annoys you or feels not right, often without a clear reason.
  17. see someone off: To go with someone to the place they are leaving from, so you can say goodbye.
  18. spill the beans: To tell a secret by accident.
  19. tag along: To go somewhere with other people who didn't ask you.
  20. take a rain check: To say no to an invitation now and suggest doing it another time.
  21. take for granted: To take something for granted means to forget how lucky you are to have it, or to expect it will always be there.
  22. water under the bridge: Something unpleasant that happened in the past and is no longer important.
  23. write off: To decide that someone or something is not worth your time or has no chance.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

bang on about

Phrasal verbLevel B2Casual

To talk about the same subject for too long, in a boring or annoying way.

Tip: Use it with 'about' plus the subject (bang on about politics, bang on about work); it's mildly critical, so say it about other people more than to their face. It's also common in the form 'go banging on about'.

AmiraHow was the train ride with Kofi? Six hours is a long time.
KenHonestly, I lost the will to live around hour two.
AmiraWhy, what happened?
KenHe wouldn't stop banging on about his new running app. Steps, routes, heart rate, everything.
AmiraHa, I'd have put my headphones on straight away.
KenI did, eventually. He didn't even notice.

Tip: Use 'chip in' with 'for' + the thing, or 'to' + a verb, and add 'with' if you name the money or help: 'chip in for a gift', 'chip in to fix it'. Don't use it for one person paying a whole bill alone.

OlenaGood idea. I'll chip in twenty, and I'll ask the others on the street.
KenGreat. I'll make a list so we know who's given what.
OlenaPerfect. Let's fix it before the next storm.

OlenaYes, last night. We're fine now, just tired.

take for granted

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

To take something for granted means to forget how lucky you are to have it, or to expect it will always be there.

Tip: Use 'take (someone/something) for granted' for things or people you stop appreciating; the object can go in the middle ('take it for granted') or you can use 'take for granted that…' for assumptions.

AmiraKen, thanks again for driving me to the airport at five in the morning.
KenNo problem. You'd do the same for me.
AmiraStill, I don't want you to think I take it for granted. It's a huge favour.
KenHonestly, I like hearing that. People usually just expect a lift.
AmiraWell, let me buy you breakfast when I'm back.
KenDeal. Text me when you land.

water under the bridge

IdiomLevel B2Casual

Something unpleasant that happened in the past and is no longer important.

Tip: Use it after a conflict or mistake to say it no longer matters; it's often said as 'that's water under the bridge' or 'it's all water under the bridge now'.

AmiraKofi, I'm sorry again about missing your moving day. I felt awful.
KofiAmira, honestly, don't worry. It's water under the bridge.
AmiraReally? You were pretty annoyed at the time.
KofiI was, but we got everything done in the end.
AmiraThanks. Let me bring dinner to the new flat on Friday.
KofiDeal. And bring a big appetite.

write off

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To decide that someone or something is not worth your time or has no chance.

Tip: Use it with a person or thing as the object: 'write someone off' or 'write it off'. A pronoun goes in the middle, so say 'write him off', not 'write off him'.

OlenaDid you see Kofi's first draft for the client pitch? It's pretty rough.
KenI did. Honestly, I was ready to write it off.
OlenaReally? He's only had two days to work on it.
KenTrue. And his last project started badly too, then it turned out great.
OlenaExactly. Let's give him until Friday and then look again.
KenFine. I'll send him some notes this afternoon.
