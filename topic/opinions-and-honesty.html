Opinions & honesty

A conversational idiom workbook: 11 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. chances are: It is likely that something is true or will happen.
  2. Don't get me wrong: A phrase you say before a 'but' to stop someone from misunderstanding you.
  3. for lack of a better way of putting it: Said when you can't find the perfect words, so you use the closest ones.
  4. I hate to break it to you: A polite way to start telling someone news they may not like.
  5. just saying: A light phrase you add after a comment to say you don't want to argue or cause trouble.
  6. pass the sniff test: To seem good, honest or believable when you check it.
  7. point blank: In a direct, straightforward way, without hesitating or being polite first.
  8. splitting hairs: Arguing about a tiny difference that is not important.
  9. to tell you the truth: A phrase you use before saying something honest, especially when it may be a little surprising.
  10. that's neither here nor there: That point is not important or not relevant to what we are discussing.
  11. You would think (that)...: Used when you expect something to be true or happen, but in reality it doesn't.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

chances are

ExpressionLevel B1Neutral

It is likely that something is true or will happen.

Tip: Use it at the start of a sentence, followed by a full clause ("Chances are he's already left"); it's a prediction, so don't use it for things you know for certain.

OlenaDid Kofi reply about Saturday? I need to know how many chairs to borrow.
MeiNot yet. He's been working late all week.
OlenaSo is he coming or not?
MeiChances are he'll come. He never misses a birthday.
OlenaOkay, I'll plan for ten people, then.

Don't get me wrong

ExpressionLevel B1Neutral

A phrase you say before a 'but' to stop someone from misunderstanding you.

Tip: Say it before you give a criticism or a different opinion, then follow it with 'but'; it softens what comes next, so don't use it for good news.

OlenaSo, what did you think of the new flat? You've been quiet since we left.
KofiDon't get me wrong, the kitchen is lovely, but the bus stop is twenty minutes away.
OlenaTrue, that's a long walk, especially in winter.
KofiAnd the rent is already at the top of what I can pay.
OlenaThen maybe keep looking. There's no rush, right?

for lack of a better way of putting it

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

Said when you can't find the perfect words, so you use the closest ones.

Tip: Put it in the middle of a sentence, usually before a description you know isn't perfect; it's often set off with commas, and don't use it when you actually have the exact word.

AmiraSo, how was the new manager's first team meeting?
KenInteresting. She's, for lack of a better way of putting it, very hands-on.
AmiraHands-on? You mean she checks everything?
KenPretty much. She asked to see our drafts before we'd even finished them.
AmiraHmm. That might get tiring after a while.
KenIt might. But she does seem to care about the work.

I hate to break it to you

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

A polite way to start telling someone news they may not like.

Tip: Say it just before the bad news, then add 'but' and the news: 'I hate to break it to you, but...'; use it with people you want to stay kind to, and don't use it for good news.

OlenaAre we still meeting Kofi at the station at six? I've got our tickets ready.
DiegoI hate to break it to you, but his train was cancelled. He just texted me.
OlenaOh no. Is he coming later, or not at all?
DiegoLater. He's on the nine o'clock one, so he'll miss the first half of the film.
OlenaOkay, let's go anyway and he can meet us for dinner afterwards.

just saying

ExpressionLevel B2Casual

A light phrase you add after a comment to say you don't want to argue or cause trouble.

Tip: Add it at the end of a comment, often with a falling tone, to soften an opinion or a warning; it's casual, so avoid it in formal writing or serious complaints.

OlenaAre you really going to leave for the airport at six? Your flight isn't until nine.
KofiYeah, I hate rushing. I'd rather wait at the gate.
OlenaFair enough. But the motorway is always packed on Friday mornings.
KofiHmm, that's true. Last time it took me almost two hours.
OlenaExactly. I'm just saying. Leave when you like, of course.
KofiNo, I'll go at five thirty. Thanks for the warning.

pass the sniff test

IdiomLevel B2Casual

To seem good, honest or believable when you check it.

Tip: Use it about claims, stories, offers or plans: 'That doesn't pass the sniff test.' Don't use it for people's smell, and don't say 'pass the sniff' on its own.

OlenaDid you see that flat listing? Two bedrooms near the station for half the usual rent.
KofiI did. The photos look great, but the owner wants a deposit before anyone can visit.
OlenaHmm. That's a bit odd.
KofiExactly. The whole thing doesn't pass the sniff test for me.
OlenaAgreed. Let's keep looking and only pay after we've seen a place in person.

point blank

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

In a direct, straightforward way, without hesitating or being polite first.

Tip: Use it with verbs of asking, telling or refusing (ask, tell, say, refuse point blank); it goes after the verb or object, and it's not used for describing a person's character.

AmiraDid you ever find out why the landlord kept putting off the repairs?
KofiYes, I got tired of the excuses, so I asked him point blank.
AmiraAnd what did he say?
KofiHe admitted he didn't have the money yet. At least I know where I stand now.
AmiraThat's better than waiting another month for an answer.

splitting hairs

IdiomLevel B2Casual

Arguing about a tiny difference that is not important.

Tip: Use it as a verb phrase: 'You're splitting hairs' or 'Let's not split hairs'; it can sound dismissive, so be careful when saying it directly to someone senior.

OlenaDid you read Kofi's report? He says sales rose by 'nearly 12 percent'.
KenIt was 11.8. He should have written 'almost 12'.
OlenaKen, you're splitting hairs. The point is that sales went up.
KenFair enough. I just like numbers to be exact.
OlenaI know, but the client only wants the big picture today.

to tell you the truth

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

A phrase you use before saying something honest, especially when it may be a little surprising.

Tip: Put it at the start of a sentence and follow it with a comma; it often introduces an opinion or a fact the other person may not expect, so keep your tone friendly.

AmiraKen, did you finish that thriller I lent you?
KenNot yet. To tell you the truth, I stopped after about fifty pages.
AmiraReally? I thought you'd love it. It gets so much better in the middle.
KenMaybe. The story just felt slow to me, and I never got interested in the characters.
AmiraThat's fine. Give it another try when you're on holiday, and if it's still not for you, just pass it on.
KenGood idea. Thanks for not being offended.

that's neither here nor there

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

That point is not important or not relevant to what we are discussing.

Tip: Use it after you mention a fact that is true but does not affect the decision, often with 'but' before it: 'It's expensive, but that's neither here nor there.' Don't use it for something that is simply false or unimportant to you personally; it means irrelevant to the topic being discussed.

OlenaI think we should book the second hotel. The reviews are better.
DiegoTrue, but it's twenty minutes from the station. That's a long walk with suitcases.
OlenaWell, we'll take a taxi anyway. And it's a bit older, but that's neither here nor there.
DiegoOkay, so what matters is the price and the breakfast.
OlenaExactly. The second one is cheaper and breakfast is included.
DiegoFine, let's book it.

You would think (that)...

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

Used when you expect something to be true or happen, but in reality it doesn't.

Tip: Follow it with a clause describing the logical expectation, then use 'but' or 'yet' to say what really happened; it often signals mild frustration or surprise.

OlenaDid you get an answer from the landlord about the broken heater?
KofiNot yet. I've emailed him three times this week.
OlenaThree times? You would think he'd reply to at least one of them.
KofiI know. It's been freezing in the flat since Monday.
OlenaMaybe try calling him. People often answer the phone faster than email.
KofiGood idea. I'll ring him after lunch.

In this conversation, what does "You would think he'd reply to at least one of them" mean?

a company that size would have a working website, but I couldn't even log in to book my appointment.

Show an example answer
