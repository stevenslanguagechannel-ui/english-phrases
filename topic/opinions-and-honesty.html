Opinions & honesty

A conversational idiom workbook: 19 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. back up: To show proof or give reasons that support what you said.
  2. chances are: It is likely that something is true or will happen.
  3. a cop-out: An excuse or choice that avoids something difficult or that you should do.
  4. Don't get me wrong: A phrase you say before a 'but' to stop someone from misunderstanding you.
  5. don't quote me on that: Used to say you are not completely sure about what you just said.
  6. for lack of a better way of putting it: Said when you can't find the perfect words, so you use the closest ones.
  7. go over well: To be received in a good way by other people.
  8. hear you out: To listen to everything someone says before you judge or decide.
  9. I hate to break it to you: A polite way to start telling someone news they may not like.
  10. I'm not going to lie: A spoken phrase that means 'to be honest' before you say something true.
  11. just saying: A light phrase you add after a comment to say you don't want to argue or cause trouble.
  12. not my cup of tea: Something you don't like or enjoy.
  13. pass the sniff test: To seem good, honest or believable when you check it.
  14. point blank: In a direct, straightforward way, without hesitating or being polite first.
  15. splitting hairs: Arguing about a tiny difference that is not important.
  16. take it with a grain of salt: Don't believe something completely, because it may not be fully true.
  17. to tell you the truth: A phrase you use before saying something honest, especially when it may be a little surprising.
  18. that's neither here nor there: That point is not important or not relevant to what we are discussing.
  19. You would think (that)...: Used when you expect something to be true or happen, but in reality it doesn't.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

back up

Phrasal verbLevel B1Neutral

To show proof or give reasons that support what you said.

Tip: Say 'back up' + what you said, or 'back it up', and often add 'with' + the proof: 'back that up with numbers'.

MeiDiego, why do you think the new schedule won't work?
DiegoHonestly, it feels too tight for the team.
MeiA feeling won't convince Olena. Can you back that up with some numbers?
DiegoYes, I tracked our hours for two weeks. I'll send you the chart.
MeiPerfect, that will help a lot.

chances are

ExpressionLevel B1Neutral

It is likely that something is true or will happen.

Tip: Use it at the start of a sentence, followed by a full clause ("Chances are he's already left"); it's a prediction, so don't use it for things you know for certain.

OlenaDid Kofi reply about Saturday? I need to know how many chairs to borrow.
MeiNot yet. He's been working late all week.
OlenaSo is he coming or not?
MeiChances are he'll come. He never misses a birthday.
OlenaOkay, I'll plan for ten people, then.

a cop-out

ExpressionLevel B2Casual

An excuse or choice that avoids something difficult or that you should do.

Tip: Use it after 'is a' or 'was a' to criticise an excuse or an easy choice; it sounds judgmental, so be careful when you say it to someone directly.

OlenaDid you tell Kofi we're not coming to his party?
DiegoNot yet. I'm going to text him that I have a headache.
OlenaHonestly, that's a bit of a cop-out.
DiegoI know, but I hate saying no to him.
OlenaHe'd prefer the truth. Just tell him we're tired and need a quiet weekend.
DiegoOkay, you're right. I'll call him tonight.

Don't get me wrong

ExpressionLevel B1Neutral

A phrase you say before a 'but' to stop someone from misunderstanding you.

Tip: Say it before you give a criticism or a different opinion, then follow it with 'but'; it softens what comes next, so don't use it for good news.

OlenaSo, what did you think of the new flat? You've been quiet since we left.
KofiDon't get me wrong, the kitchen is lovely, but the bus stop is twenty minutes away.
OlenaTrue, that's a long walk, especially in winter.
KofiAnd the rent is already at the top of what I can pay.
OlenaThen maybe keep looking. There's no rush, right?

AmiraTwo is fine. I only need to pick up some cough medicine.
KofiI walked past last month and saw the sign. Check their website first, though.
AmiraGood idea. I'll look before I leave the house.

for lack of a better way of putting it

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

Said when you can't find the perfect words, so you use the closest ones.

Tip: Put it in the middle of a sentence, usually before a description you know isn't perfect; it's often set off with commas, and don't use it when you actually have the exact word.

AmiraSo, how was the new manager's first team meeting?
KenInteresting. She's, for lack of a better way of putting it, very hands-on.
AmiraHands-on? You mean she checks everything?
KenPretty much. She asked to see our drafts before we'd even finished them.
AmiraHmm. That might get tiring after a while.
KenIt might. But she does seem to care about the work.

go over well

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

To be received in a good way by other people.

Tip: Use it with a subject that is an idea, event or message, and add an adverb like well, badly or surprisingly well: 'The new schedule went over badly.' Don't use it for people, as in 'I went over well'.

OlenaHow was the team dinner on Friday? Did people like the new restaurant?
KofiMostly, yes. The food was great, but the live music was way too loud.
OlenaOh no. I suggested that place because of the music.
KofiDon't worry. The idea of a dinner out went over well, and everyone chatted a lot anyway.
OlenaGood. Next time I'll pick somewhere quieter.

hear you out

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To listen to everything someone says before you judge or decide.

Tip: Use it as hear + person + out ("hear her out", "hear them out"); you can also say "hear me out" to ask someone to be patient before they react.

OlenaKofi, I know you hate the idea of moving the team meeting to eight o'clock.
KofiHate is a strong word, but it's very early for me. I'm taking the train in.
OlenaJust hear me out. If we meet at eight, we're done by nine and everyone gets a quiet morning.
KofiOkay, that's actually a fair point. Could we try it for two weeks?
OlenaSure. Then we can vote on whether to keep it.

I hate to break it to you

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

A polite way to start telling someone news they may not like.

Tip: Say it just before the bad news, then add 'but' and the news: 'I hate to break it to you, but...'; use it with people you want to stay kind to, and don't use it for good news.

OlenaAre we still meeting Kofi at the station at six? I've got our tickets ready.
DiegoI hate to break it to you, but his train was cancelled. He just texted me.
OlenaOh no. Is he coming later, or not at all?
DiegoLater. He's on the nine o'clock one, so he'll miss the first half of the film.
OlenaOkay, let's go anyway and he can meet us for dinner afterwards.

I'm not going to lie

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

A spoken phrase that means 'to be honest' before you say something true.

Tip: Put it before the true (often negative or surprising) thing you want to say, then a comma: "I'm not going to lie, …"; it's very common in speech, but in formal writing use "to be honest" or "frankly".

AmiraYou tried the new canteen, right? What did you think of the pasta?
DiegoI'm not going to lie, I've had better. It was a bit cold in the middle.
AmiraOh no. I was going to go there for lunch today.
DiegoMaybe try the soup instead. Other people said it was really good.
AmiraOkay, thanks. I'll give it a go.

just saying

ExpressionLevel B2Casual

A light phrase you add after a comment to say you don't want to argue or cause trouble.

Tip: Add it at the end of a comment, often with a falling tone, to soften an opinion or a warning; it's casual, so avoid it in formal writing or serious complaints.

OlenaAre you really going to leave for the airport at six? Your flight isn't until nine.
KofiYeah, I hate rushing. I'd rather wait at the gate.
OlenaFair enough. But the motorway is always packed on Friday mornings.
KofiHmm, that's true. Last time it took me almost two hours.
OlenaExactly. I'm just saying. Leave when you like, of course.
KofiNo, I'll go at five thirty. Thanks for the warning.

OlenaSure! We'll text you when we finish.

to tell you the truth

OlenaExactly. The second one is cheaper and breakfast is included.
