Work & study

A conversational idiom workbook: 16 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. breathing down your neck: Watching someone very closely, so they feel pressure.
  2. drop the ball: To make a mistake by forgetting or failing to do something you should do.
  3. get through: To finish something long or difficult.
  4. the ins and outs: The ins and outs are all the small details of how something works.
  5. leeway: Extra freedom or flexibility that lets you do something a little differently or later.
  6. a leg up: An advantage that helps you do better than others.
  7. make up (work or time): To do work or time later because you missed it earlier.
  8. on my behalf: Instead of someone, or for them, as their representative.
  9. part and parcel: Something that is a normal, necessary part of a situation or job.
  10. pick someone's brain: To ask someone who knows a lot about a subject for advice or ideas.
  11. side hustle: A small extra job you do in addition to your main job.
  12. spread yourself thin: To try to do too many things at once, so you can't do any of them well.
  13. team up: To join someone and work together to do something.
  14. toe the line: To obey the rules and do what is expected.
  15. touch base: To contact someone briefly to share news or check how things are going.
  16. use it or lose it: If you don't use a skill or something you have, you will lose it.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

breathing down your neck

IdiomLevel B2Casual

Watching someone very closely, so they feel pressure.

Tip: Use it with a person or group as the subject: 'someone is breathing down my neck' (usually while you are working on something), and don't use it for a literal breath.

MeiAre you still working on the report? It's nearly eight.
DiegoYeah, I'm stuck on the last section. The client keeps emailing me for updates.
MeiEvery hour?
DiegoPretty much. With them breathing down my neck, I can't think straight.
MeiTurn off your notifications for an hour. You'll finish faster.

drop the ball

IdiomLevel B2Casual

To make a mistake by forgetting or failing to do something you should do.

Tip: Use it as 'drop the ball (on something)': you can say 'I dropped the ball' about yourself or 'someone dropped the ball' about a team, and it is usually about a task you were responsible for, not a physical accident.

OlenaDid the landlord ever fix the heating in the flat?
DiegoNo. I said I'd call him last week, but I got busy.
OlenaIt's getting really cold in the evenings, though.
DiegoI know. I really dropped the ball on that one.
OlenaDon't worry, call him first thing tomorrow.
DiegoI will. I'll set a reminder on my phone right now.

the ins and outs

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

The ins and outs are all the small details of how something works.

Tip: Almost always used as 'the ins and outs of + noun' after verbs like learn, know, understand or explain; it's plural, so don't say 'the in and out'.

OlenaDon't worry, nobody does on day one. Who's training you?
KofiAmira. She's been there for five years, so she'll show me everything.
OlenaPerfect. Just ask lots of questions and you'll be fine.

a leg up

IdiomLevel B2Casual

An advantage that helps you do better than others.

Tip: Use it as 'give someone a leg up' or 'get a leg up'; it's friendly and common in everyday speech, but don't confuse it with 'pull someone's leg', which means to joke.

OlenaDid you hear? Kofi got the job at the design studio.
DiegoReally? That's great. He only finished his degree in June.
OlenaYes, but his uncle runs a similar company, and Kofi worked there every summer.
DiegoSo that gave him a leg up over the other candidates.
OlenaDefinitely. But he's also talented, so he deserves it.

make up (work or time)

Phrasal verbLevel B1Neutral

Tip: Use it with an object: 'make up the hours / the class / the time'; it's about doing something you missed, not inventing it, so don't confuse it with 'make something up'.

KofiI'd love to, but I was off sick on Tuesday and Wednesday.
OlenaDoes your manager care about the missed hours?
KofiA little. I need to make up the time before the weekend.
OlenaThen come in early on Thursday and finish at lunchtime on Friday.
KofiGood idea. I'll ask her this afternoon.

on my behalf

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

Instead of someone, or for them, as their representative.

Tip: Use 'on someone's behalf' (or 'on behalf of someone') before or after the verb: 'sign on my behalf', 'speak on behalf of the team'; don't confuse it with 'on behalf' used alone or with 'in favour of'.

OlenaAre you still coming to the landlord meeting on Thursday?
KofiI can't, I'll be at the airport. Could you talk to him on my behalf?
OlenaSure. What do you want me to say?
KofiJust ask when they'll fix the heating. And please write down what he says.
OlenaNo problem. I'll message you right after the meeting.

part and parcel

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

Something that is a normal, necessary part of a situation or job.

Tip: Use 'X is part and parcel of Y' (or 'come as part and parcel of Y'); it often follows a downside, and the phrase is fixed, so don't change the word order or say 'part and a parcel'.

OlenaYou look exhausted, Kofi. Another late shift at the clinic?
KofiYes, and I had three patients who were really upset today. I'm starting to wonder if I'm cut out for this.
OlenaHonestly, I think difficult days are part and parcel of nursing. Even the most experienced nurses get them.
KofiI suppose you're right. It's just harder than I expected in my first year.
OlenaIt gets easier. Go home, eat something proper and get some sleep.

pick someone's brain

IdiomLevel B2Casual

side hustle

Tip: Use it as a countable noun: 'have a side hustle', 'start a side hustle', 'do something as a side hustle'. It often suggests a way to earn extra money, and people usually say it about their own or a friend's small business.
