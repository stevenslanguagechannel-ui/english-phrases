A conversational idiom workbook: 10 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. it's on me: It is my fault or my responsibility.
  2. kick in: When something kicks in, it starts to work or take effect.
  3. the light bulb goes off: You suddenly understand something or get a good idea.
  4. make it work: To find a way to deal with a difficult situation and succeed anyway.
  5. next to impossible: Almost impossible, extremely difficult.
  6. on top of that: In addition to something already mentioned, often something else annoying or surprising.

  9. settle for: To accept something that is less good than what you really wanted.
  10. swap out: To swap something out is to remove it and put a different one in its place.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

it's on me

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

It is my fault or my responsibility.

Tip: Use it when you accept blame or take responsibility, often right after something goes wrong: 'Sorry, that's on me.' Careful: 'it's on me' can also mean 'I'm paying' (see 'the next round is on me'), so context matters.

OlenaHi Kofi, did you get the email from the client? They say the files never arrived.
KofiOh no. I think I attached the wrong folder yesterday.
OlenaReally? They need them before the meeting at three.
KofiI'm sorry, it's on me. I should have checked before I hit send.
OlenaOkay, thanks for being honest. Can you send the right files now?
KofiDoing it right now, and I'll call them to explain.

kick in

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

When something kicks in, it starts to work or take effect.

Tip: Use it for things that start working after a delay (medicine, heating, a backup system, a rule); the subject comes before it and no object follows: 'The heating kicks in at six.'

AmiraIs it still freezing in your flat?
DiegoA bit. The heating is on, but it's set to a timer.
AmiraSo when does it come on?
DiegoAround six. Once it kicks in, the whole place warms up in half an hour.
AmiraThen put a jumper on until then. It's only twenty minutes away.
DiegoGood idea. I'm making tea in the meantime.

the light bulb goes off

IdiomLevel B1Casual

You suddenly understand something or get a good idea.

Tip: Use it for a sudden moment of understanding, usually in the past tense: 'the light bulb went off'; add 'for me' or 'when I saw...' to say what triggered it.

OlenaDid you ever fix the Wi-Fi at your place?
DiegoFinally, yes. I kept restarting the router and nothing worked.
OlenaSo what changed?
DiegoMy neighbour mentioned her network name was almost the same as mine. Then the light bulb went off. I'd been connecting to hers the whole time.
OlenaHa! No wonder it was so slow.

make it work

ExpressionLevel B1Neutral

To find a way to deal with a difficult situation and succeed anyway.

Tip: Use it after a 'but' or 'even though' clause to say you'll succeed despite a limitation; it's often said as 'we'll make it work' or 'I'll make it work', and it works in formal speech too.

AmiraDid the hotel confirm the extra bed for Kofi?
DiegoNo, they only have one room left, and it's tiny.
AmiraOh no. Should we look for somewhere else?
DiegoIt's the last weekend before the conference, and everything nearby is full.
AmiraOkay, we'll take turns on the sofa. We can make it work.
DiegoFine by me. I'll tell them we're taking the room.

next to impossible

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

Almost impossible, extremely difficult.

Tip: Use it before an adjective or infinitive phrase: 'next to impossible to find' or 'next to impossible for us'; it sounds a little softer than 'impossible' because it leaves a tiny gap.

OlenaDid you manage to book a table for Saturday? It's Amira's birthday.
DiegoI tried five places. Everything is full.
OlenaEven the Italian place near the station?
DiegoYes, it's next to impossible to get a table for ten on a weekend.
OlenaThen let's skip the restaurant. I can cook at my place.
DiegoThat's a great idea. I'll bring dessert.

on top of that

ExpressionLevel B1Neutral

In addition to something already mentioned, often something else annoying or surprising.

Tip: Use it to add one more point to a list of things, usually problems; it often starts a new clause, followed by a comma, and you can also say 'on top of this' or 'on top of everything'.

AmiraHow was your move on Saturday?
DiegoHonestly, a mess. The van arrived two hours late.
AmiraOh no. Was everything okay in the end?
DiegoEventually. But the lift in the new building was broken, and on top of that, I had to carry the sofa up six floors.
AmiraSix floors? You must have been exhausted!
DiegoI could hardly walk on Sunday.

or else

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

Used to say what bad thing will happen if you don't do something.

Tip: Put 'or else' after a command or warning, then say the result: 'Do X, or else Y will happen.' It can sound like a threat if you say it firmly, so use a light tone with friends.

AmiraHave you sent the group photos to everyone yet?
KenNot yet. I'll do it after dinner.
AmiraPlease do it tonight. Upload them today, or else the shared link will expire.
KenReally? I thought it lasted a week.
AmiraIt's only seven days from when I made it, and that was last Thursday.
KenOkay, okay. I'm opening my laptop now.

put my foot down

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

To firmly refuse to allow something, or to insist on a rule.

Tip: Use it as 'put your/my/his foot down' (often with 'about' or 'when' + a situation); it suggests you were calm but firm after being patient for a while, and don't confuse it with 'put your foot in it', which means to say something embarrassing.

AmiraDid you ever sort out the noise from your upstairs neighbours?
KenNot yet. They still play loud music on weeknights, even after I asked nicely twice.
AmiraTwice? That's a lot of patience.
KenI know. Tomorrow I'm going to put my foot down and tell them it has to stop by ten o'clock.
AmiraGood. Just stay calm and be clear about it.

settle for

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To accept something that is less good than what you really wanted.

Tip: Use 'settle for' + noun or -ing (settle for a smaller flat, settle for waiting); it often follows a first choice that wasn't possible, and it's different from 'settle on', which means to finally choose something.

AmiraDid you get the laptop you wanted for university?
KofiNot exactly. The one I liked was sold out everywhere, and the next delivery isn't until January.
AmiraThat's too late. So what did you do?
KofiI settled for the older model. It's a bit slower, but it was available and it's cheaper.
AmiraHonestly, that sounds sensible. You can always upgrade later.

swap out

Phrasal verbLevel B1Neutral

To swap something out is to remove it and put a different one in its place.

Tip: Use it as 'swap out A for B' or 'swap A out for B'; with a pronoun the object must go in the middle ('swap it out', not 'swap out it').

AmiraMy laptop keeps overheating when I'm on video calls.
KenHave you checked the fan? Mine was clogged with dust.
AmiraI cleaned it, but it didn't help. The technician said the battery is the problem.
KenThen you should swap it out. A new battery isn't expensive.
AmiraTrue. I'll book it in for Saturday.
