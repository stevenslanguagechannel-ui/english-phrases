Problems & solutions

A conversational idiom workbook: 22 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. chalk it up to: To chalk something up to a reason means to say that reason explains it, often a mistake or bad luck.
  2. come down to: To be the most important reason or factor in a decision or situation.
  3. every cloud has a silver lining: Even a bad situation has something good in it.
  4. hedge your bets: To hedge your bets means to protect yourself by choosing more than one option, in case one goes wrong.
  5. if push comes to shove: If the situation becomes really difficult and you must act.
  6. in a pinch: In a difficult moment when you have no better option.
  7. in the clear: Safe from danger, trouble or blame.
  8. it's on me: It is my fault or my responsibility.
  9. keep in mind: To remember an important fact when you make a decision or plan.
  10. kick in: When something kicks in, it starts to work or take effect.
  11. the light bulb goes off: You suddenly understand something or get a good idea.
  12. make do: To manage with what you have, even if it isn't perfect.
  13. make it work: To find a way to deal with a difficult situation and succeed anyway.
  14. next to impossible: Almost impossible, extremely difficult.
  15. a no-brainer: A decision that is so easy you don't need to think about it.
  16. on top of that: In addition to something already mentioned, often something else annoying or surprising.
  17. a one-off: Something that happens only once and is not expected to happen again.
  18. or else: Used to say what bad thing will happen if you don't do something.
  19. put my foot down: To firmly refuse to allow something, or to insist on a rule.
  20. roll with the punches: To stay calm and adapt when things go wrong or change suddenly.
  21. settle for: To accept something that is less good than what you really wanted.
  22. swap out: To swap something out is to remove it and put a different one in its place.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

chalk it up to

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To chalk something up to a reason means to say that reason explains it, often a mistake or bad luck.

Tip: Use the pattern 'chalk it up to + reason' (or 'chalk X up to Y'); the reason is often a noun or an -ing phrase, as in 'chalk it up to bad luck'.

AmiraDid you fix the printer in the end?
KenNo. It printed half a page, then it just stopped.
AmiraAgain? That's the third time this week.
KenI know. I can't find anything wrong with it, so I'll chalk it up to the cold weather and try again tomorrow.
AmiraFine, but if it happens again, we're calling the repair company.

AmiraThen maybe visit for a weekend first and see how it feels.
KofiGood idea. I'll book a train tonight.

every cloud has a silver lining

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

Even a bad situation has something good in it.

Tip: Say it after a setback to point out a benefit, usually about your own situation or to comfort someone; it can sound unkind if you say it too soon to someone who is still upset.

KofiMy train was cancelled, so I'll be two hours late to the conference.
AmiraOh no, that's so annoying. Can you still make your session?
KofiNo, I'll miss it. But I'll finally have time to finish my slides on the way.
AmiraWell, every cloud has a silver lining. You were worried about them yesterday.
KofiTrue. And the station café does decent coffee, so it won't be all bad.

hedge your bets

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

To hedge your bets means to protect yourself by choosing more than one option, in case one goes wrong.

Tip: Use it as 'hedge your bets' (often 'to hedge my/our bets'), usually when you choose more than one option or avoid committing fully; it is not about gardens or fences, and it is more common in speech about plans, money and choices.

MeiHave you decided which university to go for in the autumn?
DiegoNot yet. I've applied to three, and I'm also looking at a job in case I don't get in.
MeiThat's a lot of paperwork.
DiegoI know, but I'd rather hedge my bets than end up with nothing in September.
MeiFair enough. Which one is your favourite?
DiegoThe one near the coast, but I'm keeping my options open.

it's on me

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

Tip: Use it when you accept blame or take responsibility, often right after something goes wrong: 'Sorry, that's on me.' Careful: 'it's on me' can also mean 'I'm paying' (see 'the next round is on me'), so context matters.

KofiOh no. I think I attached the wrong folder yesterday.

KofiI'm sorry, it's on me. I should have checked before I hit send.
OlenaOkay, thanks for being honest. Can you send the right files now?
KofiDoing it right now, and I'll call them to explain.

kick in

DiegoGood idea. I'm making tea in the meantime.
