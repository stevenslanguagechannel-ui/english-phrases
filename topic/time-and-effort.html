Time & effort

A conversational idiom workbook: 9 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. easy peasy, lemon squeezy: Very, very easy. It is a playful, rhyming phrase.
  2. faff about: To waste time doing small, unimportant things instead of getting on with a task.
  3. fall by the wayside: To stop being done or looked after because other things became more important.
  4. get caught up in: To get caught up in something is to become so busy with it that you forget other things.
  5. give or take: 'Give or take' means about, with a small amount more or less.
  6. go to town: To do something with great energy and enthusiasm, without holding back.
  7. lose sight of: To forget or stop thinking about what is most important.
  8. not just yet: Not now, but maybe soon.
  9. run out of time: To have no time left; to use all your time.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

easy peasy, lemon squeezy

IdiomLevel B1Casual

Very, very easy. It is a playful, rhyming phrase.

Tip: Use it after 'was/is/it's' to describe a task that turned out simple; it sounds playful and cheerful, so save it for friendly chats rather than serious or formal situations.

OlenaDid you manage to book the train tickets for Saturday?
KofiYes, all done. I was worried about the booking site, but it was easy peasy, lemon squeezy.
OlenaReally? Last time it kept crashing on me.
KofiIt took about three minutes. I even picked our seats.
OlenaGreat, then I'll let you book the hotel too.

faff about

Phrasal verbLevel C1Casual

To waste time doing small, unimportant things instead of getting on with a task.

Tip: Use 'faff about' (or 'faff around') for wasting time on small things, often with 'instead of' + -ing; it's informal and sounds a little impatient, so avoid it in formal writing.

OlenaHave you sent the booking form yet? The deadline is at five.
KofiNot yet. I spent the morning faffing about with the font and the colours.
OlenaKofi, nobody will care what it looks like. They just need the dates.
KofiYou're right. I'll fill in the details now and send it in ten minutes.
OlenaGood. Message me when it's done and I'll double-check it.

fall by the wayside

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

To stop being done or looked after because other things became more important.

Tip: Use it about habits, plans or tasks that get neglected over time, usually without anyone deciding to stop; the subject is the thing that was dropped ("my gym routine fell by the wayside"), not the person.

KenAre you still doing those Spanish lessons on your phone every morning?
OlenaHonestly, no. They fell by the wayside once the project at work got busy.
KenThat happens to me all the time. I get a streak going and then one hectic week ruins it.
OlenaExactly. I keep telling myself I'll start again on Monday.
KenTry ten minutes on the train. That's easy to keep up.

get caught up in

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To get caught up in something is to become so busy with it that you forget other things.

Tip: Use it with 'in' plus a noun or -ing form (get caught up in a conversation / in planning the trip); it often explains why you lost track of time or forgot something, and don't confuse it with 'be caught' meaning trapped or arrested.

AmiraKen, I waited for you at the station for twenty minutes. Where were you?
KenI'm so sorry! I got caught up in a work call and completely lost track of time.
AmiraI wish you'd texted me. I thought something had happened.
KenYou're right. My phone was on silent, too. Have you eaten?
AmiraNot yet. Let's just go to that noodle place by the exit.

give or take

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

'Give or take' means about, with a small amount more or less.

Tip: Put it after a number or amount, usually at the end of the phrase: 'twenty people, give or take'. Use it for estimates, not for exact facts.

OlenaHow many people are coming to the housewarming on Saturday?
KofiThirty, give or take. A few haven't replied yet.
OlenaOkay. Do we have enough chairs for that?
KofiWe have about twenty-five, so I'll ask the neighbours to lend us some.
OlenaGood idea. I'll make sure there's plenty of food too.

go to town

IdiomLevel B2Casual

To do something with great energy and enthusiasm, without holding back.

Tip: Use it as 'go to town on/with something' or 'go to town doing something'; it's informal, usually positive, and describes enthusiastic effort rather than literally travelling.

OlenaYour flat looks amazing! Did you hire someone to do the garden?
DiegoNo, I did it all myself over the weekend. Once I started, I really went to town.
OlenaI can tell. Where did all those lights and flower pots come from?
DiegoThe garden centre had a sale, so I bought way more than I needed.
OlenaWell, it was worth it. It looks like a little café out there.

lose sight of

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

To forget or stop thinking about what is most important.

Tip: Use it as lose sight of + noun (lose sight of the goal, the big picture, what matters); it often follows 'don't' or 'easy to' and works in both casual and formal speech.

OlenaYou look exhausted, Kofi. Are you still working on the kitchen yourself?
KofiYes, and I'm spending hours choosing between two almost identical shades of white paint.
OlenaBe careful you don't lose sight of why you started. You wanted a bright, simple room.
KofiYou're right. Honestly, either colour would look fine.
OlenaThen pick one tonight and start painting tomorrow.

not just yet

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

Not now, but maybe soon.

Tip: Use it as a short reply when someone asks if you're ready or if something can start; it often comes before a reason, and 'just yet' makes the delay feel brief.

OlenaHey Kofi, are you ready to leave? The taxi's outside.
KofiNot just yet. I can't find my charger.
OlenaIs it in your bag?
KofiI checked there. Oh, wait, it's under the sofa. Got it!
OlenaGreat. Two minutes, then we go?
KofiTwo minutes. I just need to grab my coat.

run out of time

Phrasal verbLevel B1Neutral

To have no time left; to use all your time.

Tip: Use 'run out of time' (no object) to say the time is finished, and 'run out of + noun' for other things (run out of money, run out of milk); don't confuse it with 'be out of time', which sounds more final.

OlenaHow did the presentation go this morning?
KenNot great, honestly. I spent too long on the first few slides.
OlenaOh no. Did you get to the end?
KenNot really. I ran out of time before I could show the results.
OlenaThat's a shame, those were the best part. Maybe you can send them by email.
KenGood idea. I'll do that after lunch.
