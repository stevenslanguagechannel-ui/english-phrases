Feelings & reactions

A conversational idiom workbook: 8 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. get sick of: To become tired of something or someone because you have had too much of it.
  2. a hard pill to swallow: A fact or piece of news that is difficult or upsetting to accept.
  3. hot under the collar: Angry or annoyed, often over something small.
  4. I could get used to this: I like this a lot and would be happy if it happened all the time.
  5. not feeling it: You have no energy, mood or motivation to do something.
  6. put up with: To accept something annoying without complaining or leaving.
  7. take it in stride: To accept a problem calmly without getting upset.
  8. under your breath: In a very quiet voice, so other people cannot hear well.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

get sick of

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

To become tired of something or someone because you have had too much of it.

Tip: Use 'get sick of' + noun or -ing (get sick of waiting); it shows real frustration, so save it for informal talk and avoid it with someone you don't know well.

DiegoAre you still eating the same lunch every day?
MeiYes, chicken salad. It's quick, and I don't have time to cook.
DiegoAren't you bored of it by now?
MeiHonestly, I'm about to get sick of it. I might try the new noodle place tomorrow.
DiegoGood idea. I'll come with you.

a hard pill to swallow

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

A fact or piece of news that is difficult or upsetting to accept.

Tip: Say 'X was a hard pill to swallow' about news or a result you had to accept, not about a person; it often follows a disappointment, and you can add 'but' to show you have moved on.

AmiraDid you hear back from the housing office about your flat?
KofiYes. They're knocking the building down next spring, so everyone has to move out.
AmiraOh no. After six years there?
KofiI know. It's a hard pill to swallow, honestly. I really loved that place.
AmiraI'm sorry. Do you want help looking at new apartments this weekend?
KofiThat would be great. Thanks.

hot under the collar

IdiomLevel B2Casual

Angry or annoyed, often over something small.

Tip: Use it with get or be ("he gets hot under the collar"), usually about a person's reaction to something minor; it sounds odd for serious anger.

AmiraDid you see Ken's reply to the group email about the meeting room?
DiegoI did. All we asked was whether he could swap rooms on Thursday.
AmiraI know. He got really hot under the collar about it.
DiegoMaybe he's just stressed. He has that deadline on Friday.
AmiraTrue. I'll bring him a coffee and say sorry for the short notice.
DiegoGood idea. That should calm things down.

I could get used to this

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

I like this a lot and would be happy if it happened all the time.

Tip: Say it when you enjoy something new and wish it could be normal; it's often a light, slightly joking comment, and it's followed by 'this' or 'it', not by a noun.

AmiraHow was your first week working from the new office?
KofiHonestly, great. It's a ten-minute walk, and there's a café downstairs.
AmiraThat's a big change from your two-hour commute.
KofiI know. I got home at six yesterday and still had energy to cook. I could get used to this.
AmiraJust don't get too comfortable. Wait until the rain starts!

not feeling it

ExpressionLevel B1Casual

You have no energy, mood or motivation to do something.

Tip: Say 'I'm not feeling it' (or 'she's not feeling it') when you lack the mood or energy for something; it's casual, so use it with friends and colleagues, not in a formal email.

KofiAre you still coming to the gym with us after work?
AmiraI don't think so. I've had such a long day.
KofiCome on, you said you'd go this week.
AmiraI know, and I wanted to. But I'm really not feeling it tonight.
KofiFair enough. We'll go on Thursday instead, then.
AmiraPerfect. I'll be much more energetic by then.

put up with

Phrasal verbLevel B1Neutral

To accept something annoying without complaining or leaving.

Tip: Use 'put up with' + noun or -ing (put up with the delay, put up with waiting); it often shows that something is annoying, and it is common in negative sentences and questions with 'how'.

AmiraAre you still living with those flatmates from the ad?
KofiYes, but I'm looking for a new place. They leave dirty dishes everywhere.
AmiraUgh. Why don't you just tell them to stop?
KofiI did, twice. Nothing changed. I've had enough.
AmiraI don't know how you've put up with it for so long.
KofiMe neither. My lease ends in March, so I'm counting the days.

take it in stride

IdiomLevel B2Neutral

To accept a problem calmly without getting upset.

Tip: Use it as 'take + it/something + in stride' for a setback or surprise, e.g. 'She took the delay in stride'; the phrase is common in American English and also well understood in British English.

AmiraDid you hear? The landlord is doubling the work on our flat, so we'll have builders here all month.
KofiOh no. That sounds really stressful. How are you feeling about it?
AmiraHonestly, I'm fine. I'll just work from the library until it's done.
KofiYou're taking it in stride. I'd be complaining to everyone I know.
AmiraI did that for the first hour, then I decided it wasn't helping.
KofiFair enough. Let me know if you need a quiet desk at my office.

under your breath

ExpressionLevel B2Neutral

In a very quiet voice, so other people cannot hear well.

Tip: Use it after a verb of speaking, such as say, mutter, mumble or swear: 'She muttered something under her breath.' It usually suggests the person is annoyed or doesn't want to be heard, so don't use it for friendly whispering.

OlenaDid you see Kofi's face when the manager changed the deadline again?
KenI did. He smiled, but he was saying something under his breath the whole time.
OlenaCould you hear what it was?
KenNot really. I was standing too far away, but it didn't look polite.
OlenaWell, I don't blame him. That's the third change this week.
