Feelings & reactions

A conversational idiom workbook: 29 phrases, each with a short conversation and four quick tasks. Do it online and check your answers, or print it and use the answer key at the end.

In this workbook

  1. blow over: To pass and end without causing serious trouble.
  2. cabin fever: The restless, bored feeling you get from staying inside for too long.
  3. cool as a cucumber: Very calm, even in a stressful situation.
  4. crack up: To suddenly laugh a lot.
  5. cry over spilled milk: To be upset about something bad that already happened and can't be changed.
  6. drive someone up the wall: To annoy someone very much.
  7. fed up with: Annoyed and tired of something that keeps happening.
  8. feel run down: To feel tired and a little unwell.
  9. fire back: To answer quickly and sharply, usually when you disagree or feel criticised.
  10. get cold feet: To suddenly feel too nervous to do something you planned.
  11. get over: To become well again after an illness or a bad time.
  12. get sick of: To become tired of something or someone because you have had too much of it.
  13. the grass is always greener on the other side: Other people's lives or things always look better than your own.
  14. a hard pill to swallow: A fact or piece of news that is difficult or upsetting to accept.
  15. have a blast: To enjoy yourself a lot.
  16. hold your horses: You say 'hold your horses' to tell someone to wait or slow down.
  17. hot under the collar: Angry or annoyed, often over something small.
  18. I could get used to this: I like this a lot and would be happy if it happened all the time.
  19. let your hair down: To relax and have fun without worrying about being serious.
  20. no harm, no foul: Nothing bad happened, so there is no need to worry or blame anyone.
  21. not feeling it: You have no energy, mood or motivation to do something.
  22. out of nowhere: Suddenly and unexpectedly, with no warning.
  23. put up with: To accept something annoying without complaining or leaving.
  24. a storm in a teacup: A big fuss about something that is not really important.
  25. strike a chord: To make people feel something because it matches their own feelings or experience.
  26. take it in stride: To accept a problem calmly without getting upset.
  27. under your breath: In a very quiet voice, so other people cannot hear well.
  28. What's the worst that could happen?: A rhetorical question that means the risk is small, so it's fine to try.
  29. You don't say!: An expression used to show genuine surprise, or sarcastically to reply to something very obvious.

How to use it: read the conversation out loud first, then do the four tasks. For the last task, answer out loud before you write, and try to use the phrase naturally.

blow over

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To pass and end without causing serious trouble.

Tip: Use 'blow over' for arguments, scandals, storms or awkward moments that fade without lasting damage; the subject is the problem, not a person (say 'the row will blow over', not 'she will blow over').

AmiraDid you see Ken's face in the meeting? He was furious about the schedule change.
DiegoI did. Should I go and talk to him?
AmiraI'd give him a day. These things usually blow over once people have had time to cool down.
DiegoTrue. Last time he was upset, he was laughing about it by lunch.
AmiraExactly. Just don't bring it up again this afternoon.

cabin fever

IdiomLevel B2Casual

The restless, bored feeling you get from staying inside for too long.

Tip: Say 'I've got cabin fever' or 'I'm getting cabin fever' for yourself; it's used for being stuck indoors (bad weather, illness, lockdown), not for feeling bored at work or in a busy place.

KofiYou look tired, Olena. Did you sleep badly?
OlenaA bit. I've been working from my flat all week because of the storm.
KofiHave you been out at all?
OlenaOnly to the bin. I think I'm getting cabin fever, honestly. I keep tidying shelves just to move around.
KofiCome to the café with me tomorrow. The rain should stop by the morning.
OlenaYes, please. I need to see other people.

cool as a cucumber

IdiomLevel B2Casual

OlenaWow, I'd have frozen on the spot.

cry over spilled milk

IdiomLevel B1Casual

To be upset about something bad that already happened and can't be changed.

Tip: Use it in the pattern 'no use crying over spilled milk' or 'there's no point crying over spilled milk' to comfort someone, but avoid saying it to a person who is still very upset, because it can sound dismissive.

AmiraDid you check when the next one leaves?
Kofi8:40. I keep thinking I should have left the house ten minutes earlier.
AmiraWell, it's too late now. There's no point crying over spilled milk.
KofiYou're right. I'll message the organiser and grab a coffee at the station.

fire back

Phrasal verbLevel B2Neutral

To answer quickly and sharply, usually when you disagree or feel criticised.

Tip: Use 'fire back' with 'with' + what was said, or a quoted reply: 'She fired back with a quick message.' It suggests the reply was fast and a bit sharp, so don't use it for a calm, thoughtful answer.

AmiraDid you see Ken's message in the group chat? He said our team always hands in work late.
DiegoI did. Olena was really annoyed by it.
AmiraDid she answer him?
DiegoYes, within a minute. She fired back that his own reports are never ready on time.
AmiraOh no. I hope they sort it out before Monday's meeting.

get cold feet

IdiomLevel B1Casual

To suddenly feel too nervous to do something you planned.

IdiomLevel B2Casual

Other people's lives or things always look better than your own.

Tip: Say it when someone is tempted by, or envies, something they don't have; it often warns that the other option may not be as good as it looks, and it is usually followed by a comment about the problems you can't see.

KofiMaybe. But I think the grass is always greener on the other side, you know?
OlenaOkay, fair. I do like chatting with my team over lunch.

hold your horses

IdiomLevel B1Casual

You say 'hold your horses' to tell someone to wait or slow down.

Tip: Say it as a command, usually with an exclamation, to someone who is rushing or jumping to conclusions; it is friendly between equals but can sound bossy to a manager or a stranger.

OlenaHold your horses, Kofi. We haven't checked the cancellation terms yet.
KofiOh, you're right. I was in such a hurry to finish.
OlenaIt's only page nine. Give me ten minutes and we'll go through it together.
KofiFine. I'll make us some tea while you read.
